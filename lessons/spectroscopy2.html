Heidelberg · 1859

The spectroscope bench of Professors Bunsen and Kirchhoff
You are in the laboratory. Take things down from the shelves and set them up on the bench: a lamp at the back, then burners or glowing tubes in the path of its light. To colour a burner’s flame, drag a salt jar onto it (a platinum wire carries a pinch into the flame). The light runs into the spectroscope on the right, a prism in a cigar box between two old telescope tubes, and you look through the eyepiece below. (You can also click a thing, then click a stand.)
Point at anything on the shelves to read its label.

Reference cards:
The rule we found. A glowing gas or flame gives bright lines at its own colours. Put a brighter light behind it and the same lines turn dark, because it soaks up more of those colours than it gives out. Put a dimmer light behind it and they stay bright. Each element always shows the same lines.
Wavelengths are given in modern nanometres (nm), a unit nobody used in 1859; Kirchhoff read his lines off an arbitrary scale.
Line positions are measured values. Brightnesses are simplified, and real lines are far thinner than drawn.
Helium, neon and argon sit on the shelf for comparison: they were not discovered until 1868 to 1898.
