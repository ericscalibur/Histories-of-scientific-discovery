BERLIN · 1914

PHYSIKALISCHES INSTITUT · JAMES FRANCK & GUSTAV HERTZ
The question. Shoot electrons through mercury vapour. When an electron hits a mercury atom, how much of its energy can it hand over? Any amount, as a ball would to another ball? Or only certain amounts? Franck and Hertz built a glass tube with a hot wire that gives off electrons, a wire grid that pulls them along, and a plate that catches the ones still moving. Then they turned up the voltage, step by step, and watched a very sensitive meter.

What the drops mean

An electron with less than 4.9 electron-volts of energy just bounces off mercury atoms and keeps going. The moment it reaches 4.9, it can hand exactly that much to an atom, and stops. It cannot give 2 or 3; it is 4.9 or nothing. So each time the voltage passes another 4.9 volts, a new batch of electrons is stopped short of the plate, and the current drops.

Energy inside an atom comes in fixed steps. Niels Bohr had proposed exactly that for hydrogen in 1913; here it was, measured with a voltmeter.

The light that gives it away

The atom does not keep the energy. It gives it straight back as a flash of light of one colour: 1240 ÷ 4.9 ≈ 253 nm, ultraviolet. Mercury’s strongest spectral line is at 253.7 nm. Later in 1914, Franck and Hertz looked through a quartz window and found that line appearing only once the voltage passed 4.9 V.

They read it wrong at first

The title of their 1914 paper calls 4.9 V the Ionisierungsspannung: the voltage needed to knock an electron right out of mercury. In 1915 Bohr argued it was the energy to lift mercury’s electron one step up, not out. He was right; the true ionisation energy of mercury is about 10.4 V. Franck and Hertz shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1925.
